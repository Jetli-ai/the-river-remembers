# The River Remembers — Film Blueprint

## Logline

Days before his investiture, Crown Prince Tayo backs a new canal that will secure water for the capital’s palace district. Nia Adeyemi, a river engineer returning to bury her father, proves the plan repeats the diversion that destroyed her home quarter and ruined her father’s name. As they race to reopen an old flood channel, Tayo and Nia uncover a truth neither family can keep: the former king ordered the diversion, while Nia’s father signed a false report to protect the workers who carried it out. Their only way forward is to tell the whole story and let the river’s people decide what repair means.

## Tone and promise

A romantic civic drama with the scale and emotional clarity of a modern royal melodrama. The palace is beautiful, but its beauty does not make its decisions wise. The river is not magical; it is a living system whose consequences expose human choices. Conflict comes from conflicting duties, incomplete evidence, public pressure, and the cost of honesty.

The film moves from intimate objects and faces to public spaces and water infrastructure. Performance stays natural and emotionally readable. Warm gold and clay tones belong to palace and market life; cooler dawn and storm light track the river crisis. Dialogue is direct, with room for silence where a character has to decide whether to tell the truth. Avoid ornamental exoticism, caricature, and generic visual shorthand. Build a coherent fictional culture through specific architecture, textiles, food, music, and civic rituals, and keep those choices consistent.

## Themes

- Leadership is stewardship, measured by who is protected when resources are scarce.
- A lie told to preserve peace can become an inheritance of harm.
- Accountability includes context, but context does not erase responsibility.
- Forgiveness can make room for repair without requiring anyone to forget.
- Love is the courage to see another person clearly and still expect them to answer for their choices.
- Water, like trust, cannot be owned by one household without consequence to all.

## Main characters

**NIA ADEYEMI, 30 —** River systems engineer; precise, compassionate, stubborn about evidence. She left Aroko after her father was publicly blamed for the old diversion. She does not want a palace apology as a substitute for repair. Her arc is from defending her father’s memory at any cost to accepting a fuller truth, including his compromise, while insisting that the living community has the final say.

**CROWN PRINCE TAYO ADEREMI, 32 —** Heir to Aroko’s throne; thoughtful, disciplined, and accustomed to carrying decisions before he understands their consequences. He returned believing a technically sound canal could end the drought. His arc is from treating authority as the ability to act quickly to understanding it as the willingness to share power, disclose failure, and accept public judgment.

**QUEEN MOTHER SERA, 59 —** Tayo’s mother and the late king’s closest adviser. She preserved the official account of the flood, partly to prevent a fragile peace from collapsing. She knows the lie has outlived its purpose. Her late admission can open a door, but she cannot choose the truth’s consequences for others.

**BABA KUNLE, 67 —** Retired gatekeeper who worked beside Nia’s father. Patient, practical, and wary of heroic stories. He remembers that frightened people make bargains in storms. He offers testimony only when Nia agrees to hear the part that hurts her.

**CHIEF MINISTER BAKO, 54 —** The palace’s chief administrator. He supported the emergency diversion and later helped turn an incomplete report into a clean official story. He is not a mastermind; he believes stability is a public good and fears the harm that disclosure may unleash. His defensiveness becomes a final choice: continue hiding behind procedure or testify to what he signed.

**MARA, 28 —** Market cooperative chair and Nia’s oldest friend. Wry, organized, and unimpressed by ceremonial promises. She keeps the community’s repair ledger and challenges both Nia and Tayo when they speak as if the neighborhood is only a symbol.

All characters are adults. No real person is depicted or imitated.

## Story synopsis

### Act I — The waterline (00:00–11:45)

At first light, a warning bell rings over the old floodgate. Water presses against a timber brace while a work crew waits for an order. A hand stamps the palace seal on a paper marked “temporary diversion.” Before the gate opens, cut to black.

Nia returns to Aroko for her father’s burial. The drought has lowered the river and exposed the old channel he was blamed for closing. At the same time, the palace posts a plan for a new canal. It will protect the upper district and the royal gardens, but the route runs through the market quarter where Nia grew up.

Tayo presents the canal to the council as an urgent, engineered solution. Nia interrupts with survey maps: the canal’s intake sits above a weak embankment, and the old channel could carry floodwater if repaired. Tayo sees a risk to the only funded plan; Nia sees another community asked to pay for a decision made elsewhere. Their argument is sharp, professional, and personal without turning into instant attraction.

Mara lets Nia stay above the market cooperative’s workshop. The residents are tired of being promised future compensation. Nia asks for access to the royal archive’s flood records. Bako refuses: the files are sealed, and the investiture is only days away. Queen Mother Sera quietly watches the exchange.

### Act II — The buried channel (11:45–35:15)

Tayo visits the river to inspect the proposed intake. Nia is already there, measuring the current with a rope and a weighted tin. Rather than dismiss her, he asks her to show him where the plan fails. She points to a narrow section where sediment has shifted the bank. The project is technically possible, but its safety margin depends on displacing the market and on holding back the old flood channel.

Tayo delays the canal vote and gives Nia one day with the archive. In a drawer of flood maps, she finds two versions of the same report. The public version says her father, a gatekeeper, closed the channel without authorization. A marked copy shows the palace ordered a diversion during a storm. One signature is missing.

Baba Kunle leads Nia and Tayo to the old gate. He explains that the workers faced a choice between directing water toward the palace granary or toward the lower quarter. Nia’s father followed an order to open a temporary spillway, saving the granary and the workers’ homes but flooding the market. Later, he signed a statement that made the decision look like his alone. Kunle admits he also signed. The decision saved lives; the cover-up stole the community’s right to understand what happened and rebuild safely.

Nia is angry that her father’s name cannot be made spotless. Tayo is angry that his family’s seal cannot be made harmless. Their trust deepens through work, not rescue: they survey the old channel, repair a test gate, and listen as residents describe what flood and drought have cost them. They share a quiet meal in the workshop after a long day. Tayo tells Nia he wants to be a king who never has to apologize; she tells him that is how people learn to hide things from him.

Sera asks Nia to meet alone and gives her a sealed ledger and an audio message recorded by the late king. The ledger shows that the palace knew the old channel needed repair and diverted its maintenance funds for a ceremonial expansion. The recording contains the king’s order to redirect the flood and his fear of panic. Sera admits she chose to protect the dynasty’s reputation. Nia will not promise to keep the secret. Tayo hears enough to realize his mother has withheld evidence from him too.

Bako moves the investiture forward and announces the canal as a symbol of unity. He releases a partial report that paints Nia as a politically motivated obstructionist. The market cooperative divides: some want immediate jobs and compensation; others refuse a deal that leaves their homes exposed. Mara tells Nia that being right about the river does not give her the right to decide for everyone.

Nia and Tayo fall out. He asks for a private statement so he can stop the canal without destabilizing the palace. She refuses a private truth designed to preserve public silence. Tayo asks her to trust him. Nia answers: “Trust is not a gift you request. It is a record you build.”

A storm approaches earlier than expected. The temporary canal berm begins to fail. Bako’s guards close the river road to protect the palace grounds, trapping people and market carts on the lower bank. Tayo can order the gates opened, but the old channel is partly blocked and untested. Nia proposes a controlled release through the original channel, which will flood the palace garden and may damage the royal archive. Tayo chooses the people over the building and publicly orders the gates opened. Bako hesitates, then hands Tayo the key to the old control room.

### Act III — The open gates (35:15–47:21)

Nia, Tayo, Kunle, and the market crew clear the channel by hand and with a small service boat. The sequence is urgent but human-scale: a stuck chain, rising water, a child separated from her aunt, the old gate groaning under pressure. Nia directs the release. Tayo takes responsibility for the flooded garden and stays with the crew rather than returning to the palace.

The water runs into the old channel. It misses the market, fills the public reservoir, and carries silt into the palace garden. No one calls it a miracle. It works because people chose a route that distributed the risk and because the gate was maintained by the hands the palace had excluded.

At the emergency assembly, Tayo puts the ledger and recording before the public. He names the former king’s order, his mother’s concealment, Bako’s role, and the market’s loss. He also names his own mistake: he nearly repeated the diversion because he trusted a map that counted land but not the people living on it.

Nia tells the truth about her father’s signature and refuses to turn him into a saint or a villain. Kunle confirms the workers’ part. Bako testifies to what he signed. The residents vote to suspend the canal, establish a river council with neighborhood seats, publish all water contracts, and begin repairs before compensation talks. Tayo does not receive instant forgiveness. He accepts a public review of the crown’s authority before his investiture.

Months later, the market opens beside a repaired channel. Nia leads the independent river council. Tayo arrives without guards to ask whether she will walk with him—not as a royal command and not as a promise that their disagreements have ended. She says yes, but keeps the repair ledger between them. They laugh, then turn together toward the sound of water. The river remembers every choice; the people decide what to do with that memory.

## Dramatic guardrails

- The central conflict is civic trust and shared water; romance remains a human subplot.
- No character is wise only because they are poor, royal, old, or young. Give every lead competing motives and the capacity to change.
- The crown is not absolved by a single confession. Show public process and continued accountability.
- Nia does not become a reward for Tayo’s growth. She retains her vocation, voice, and independent standing.
- The father’s good deed does not erase his false signature; his mistake does not erase the lives his action saved.
- The storm is a test of prior choices, not a supernatural solution.
- End with repair underway, not with every social problem solved.
